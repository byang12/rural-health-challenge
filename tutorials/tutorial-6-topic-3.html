Writing a convincing proposal

Reading time: about 5 minutes

In this topic

Write a decision-first argument that explains why your solution is sound and how it affects the rural clinic.

You will leave with a revised three-sentence recommendation and impact statements that claim no more than your evidence shows.

What “convincing” means here

A convincing proposal is not the one with the most data or the boldest claims. It is the one a busy reader can follow, check, and act on. The reader should see what you recommend, why, what it costs, and what remains uncertain, without hunting for any of it.

Practice case: a first draft

“Rural clinics face many pressures, and AI is changing healthcare. We analyzed referral data and found that our AI tool cuts processing time by 30%, saves the clinic $10 a week, and helps patients get care faster. The clinic should adopt it.”

This draft has a recommendation and real calculations behind it, yet a careful reader would not trust it. The principles below explain why, and the revision at the end of this topic fixes each problem.

Five principles to follow

1. Lead with the conclusion

Put the bottom line up front. State your recommendation on page 1 so a busy hospital administrator knows at once what you propose. Background should support the decision, not lead up to it. Leading with the decision is not the same as overstating it: a decision-first opening can still be conditional.

2. Use plain language

Write so clinicians, business managers, and community leaders can all follow the logic. Use short sentences. Define a technical or AI term only when the decision depends on it, such as “handling time includes staff review.” Say what a tool does instead of naming a category: “the tool fills in the referral form, and staff check it” is clearer than “AI-driven automation.”

3. Say what kind of number it is

Label each input as observed, estimated, or assumed, and each result as measured or modeled. Put the label in the sentence and the source in a table note or your spreadsheet, so a reader can trace every main claim.

Before

“Our AI tool cuts processing time by 30%.”

After

“Under the teaching assumptions (20 packets per week; 10 minutes manual and 7 minutes assisted, including review), the assisted workflow would use 140 instead of 200 staff minutes per week.”

4. Separate staff time from cash

Freed staff time gives the clinic capacity to do more, such as patient follow-up. It does not lower payroll unless staffing changes. A software subscription is actual cash spending. Report each with its own unit instead of combining them into one “savings” figure.

Before

“Saves the clinic $10 a week.”

After

“The subscription adds $20 a week in cash spending, and payroll does not change. The freed hour is potential staff capacity, valued at about $30 at an illustrative rate.”

5. Propose a realistic next step

Frame the recommendation around a small, low-risk check, not a clinic-wide overhaul on day one. A useful next step says who does what, for how long, what is measured, and which result would change the decision. “Adopt the tool” and “run a county-wide study” are not steps a partner can assess quickly.

Three pitfalls to avoid

Pitfall: the “mystery novel”

Four pages about how the team cleaned the data, told in the order it happened, with the recommendation hidden on the last page.

Instead: keep the chronological story in your Team Log (Topic 5). The proposal reorders it so the decision comes first.

Pitfall: overclaiming

Claiming that 60 minutes of freed staff time automatically cuts payroll by $30 a week, or that the change guarantees better patient health.

Instead: claim only what your evidence shows, and name the evidence a larger claim would need. Showing that referrals are completed sooner, for example, would require completion times before and after the change. Processing time alone is not enough.

Pitfall: the data dump

Pasting raw tables, code, or county statistics that do not help answer the clinic’s question.

Instead: make every table or figure in the proposal support one point about the recommendation. Keep the rest in your supporting artifact, or leave it out.

The revision

First draft

“Rural clinics face many pressures, and AI is changing healthcare. We analyzed referral data and found that our AI tool cuts processing time by 30%, saves the clinic $10 a week, and helps patients get care faster. The clinic should adopt it.”

Revised

“We propose an assisted workflow for preparing referral packets, with staff reviewing every packet. Under the teaching assumptions, it would use about 140 instead of 200 staff minutes per week, freeing about one hour of staff capacity; the subscription adds $20 a week in cash spending, and payroll does not change. Before any decision, we recommend timing one typical week of packets to check whether handling stays below about 8 minutes per packet, the point at which the freed time no longer covers the subscription.”

What changed:

  • The decision comes first, and the general background is gone.
  • The numbers are labeled and can be traced to their inputs.
  • Cash spending and staff capacity are reported separately.
  • The claim about patients is removed because nothing in the case supports it. It can return as something to test.
  • “Adopt it” becomes a small check with a stated decision point.
  • “Our AI tool” becomes a description of what the workflow does, with staff review included.

Practice activity

Try it: revise your recommendation

Write your recommendation in three sentences: the proposed change, the strongest evidence, and the next validation step. Then rewrite one impact statement so it names its unit, its type (measured, modeled, or assumed), and whether it is cash, staff capacity, or community access. Ask someone outside your field to explain both back to you, and revise any term they stumble on.

Check your writing

  • Could a reader find your recommendation in the first paragraph?
  • Is every important number labeled and traceable to a source or calculation?
  • Are cash spending, staff capacity, and community effects stated separately?
  • Is there any claim your evidence does not support?
  • Is the next step small enough for a partner to assess?
Suggested reading

Shah et al. (R7) for separating a workflow improvement from a financial benefit, and Ortiz and Wan (R10) for efficiency alongside community value.
