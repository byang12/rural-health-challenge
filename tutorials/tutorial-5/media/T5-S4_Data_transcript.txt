Section 4: Data you can use with AI
Tutorial 5: AI Applications and Scenario Testing

AI tools run on data, including whatever you type into them. This section is about what's safe to share, what never is, and where to find good data for practice.

When you type into a public AI tool, your words leave your device. Depending on the tool and your settings, they may be stored, used to improve the tool, or read by human reviewers. For example, Google says reviewed Gemini chats can be kept for up to three years.

Here's a simple rule: think of a traffic light. Green means go: public statistics, published reports, and made-up practice examples. Yellow means check first: internal information that isn't about patients, like a draft schedule. Red means never: anything about a real patient, other organizations' confidential files, and passwords.

Removing names isn't enough. One famous study estimated that 87 percent of Americans were likely unique, based on just their ZIP code, birth date, and sex.

In small rural communities, the risk is even higher, because fewer people share any detail. That's why, under the federal rule for removing identifiers from health data, only the first three digits of a ZIP code may stay, and only if that area has more than 20,000 people. And it's why the CDC's public data tool hides counts below ten.

So where can you practice? With public data. For example, CDC PLACES has local health estimates for nearly every county. The USDA's rural-urban codes show how rural each county is. And HRSA's data warehouse maps areas short of health workers.

When you need examples that look like patient records, use made-up ones. Write your own, with obviously fake names and details. Or use free synthetic data, such as Synthea, which generates realistic but fictional patient records.

Before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask three questions. Is it public, or made up? Could it identify a person, even without a name? Would I be comfortable if someone else read it? If you're not sure, don't paste it.

Public, summary, or made-up data: yes. Real patient records: never. Next, you'll put this into practice with a real AI tool.
