What is AI, and what does it do in health care? Let's cover the core idea, how hospitals use it, and four warning signs.

Most AI isn't programmed rule by rule. It learns patterns from examples. A spam filter studies thousands of emails marked spam or not spam, then makes a guess about each new one.

AI tools mostly do one of two jobs. Predictive AI sorts, scores, or flags. Generative AI creates new content, like text or images. Chat tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini are generative AI.

Prediction tools are already common in hospitals. In 2023, about two in three U.S. hospitals used AI or other prediction tools in their electronic health records, most often to flag patients who might get worse, or who need follow-up care.

Some tools read medical images. In 2018, the FDA authorized an AI system that checks eye photos for diabetes-related eye disease in primary care offices, with no eye specialist needed to read the photos.

Generative AI is newer. It can draft clinical notes, or replies to patient messages, for a person to review. In 2024, about one in three U.S. hospitals reported using it.

Now, four warning signs. One: the data it learned from may not fit your patients. A tool built in one kind of hospital can lose accuracy in another, with different people, equipment, or records.

Two: it can aim at the wrong target. A widely used tool picked patients for extra care by predicting costs, not health. Less was spent on equally sick Black patients, so it passed over many of them. Researchers estimated that without this bias, Black patients' share of those picked would have risen from about 18 to 47 percent.

Three: it can make things up, and still sound sure. In a 2023 study, more than half the references from an early version of ChatGPT were fake. A newer version still invented almost one in five.

Four: people tend to over-trust it. In a review of studies, wrong advice from a computer tool made wrong decisions about 26 percent more likely than with no tool at all. So a trained person must check it, and make the call.

So: AI learns patterns from examples, then predicts or creates. It's useful, and it has limits. Next: what makes rural health care different, and why that matters for AI.

The core idea

Most AI is not programmed rule by rule. Instead, it learns patterns from many examples. Then it uses those patterns to make a guess about something new. A spam filter, for instance, studies thousands of emails that people have marked "spam" or "not spam." When a new email arrives, it guesses which kind it is.

That one idea explains why AI is useful and also why it can go wrong. An AI tool is only as good as the examples it learned from and the question it was built to answer.

Key terms (for reference)

  • Artificial intelligence (AI)

    Software that does tasks that usually take human judgment, such as sorting, predicting, or writing.

  • Machine learning

    The most common way to build AI. The computer finds patterns in examples instead of following rules a person wrote.

  • Training data

    The examples an AI tool learns from.

  • Predictive AI

    AI that sorts, scores, or flags. Example: "This patient may get worse in the next day."

  • Generative AI

    AI that creates new content, such as text or images.

  • Large language model (LLM)

    The kind of generative AI behind chat tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini. It writes by predicting likely next words.

  • Hallucination

    When generative AI states something false, or invents a source, as if it were true.

  • Electronic health record (EHR)

    The digital chart where a hospital or clinic keeps patients' health information.

Four warning signs, in more depth

  1. 1

    The data may not fit your patients

    A tool learns from the patients, equipment, and record-keeping habits of the places that built it. When any of these change, its accuracy can drop. Researchers call this dataset shift. A new camera, a different mix of patients, or a new way of writing notes can all cause it (Finlayson 2021).

  2. 2

    It can aim at the wrong target

    Large health systems used a widely sold tool to pick patients for extra care programs. The tool predicted how much each patient's care would cost, as a stand-in for how sick they were. But less money had been spent on Black patients who were just as sick, so the tool rated them as healthier than they were. The researchers estimated that removing this bias would raise Black patients' share of those picked for extra help from about 18% to 47%. By industry estimates, tools of this type are applied to about 200 million people in the U.S. each year. When the researchers tested a version that combined predicted health with predicted cost, the bias they measured dropped by 84% (Obermeyer 2019). The lesson: always ask what a tool actually predicts.

  3. 3

    It can make things up, and still sound sure

    Generative AI writes what sounds likely, not what has been checked. In a 2023 study, 55% of the references written by an early version of ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) were fabricated. A newer version (GPT-4) still fabricated 18% (Walters 2023). Never cite a source you haven't found and opened yourself.

  4. 4

    People tend to over-trust it

    This is called automation bias. In a review of studies, wrong advice from a computer decision-support tool made a wrong decision about 26% more likely than having no tool at all. Over-trust grows with heavy workload, time pressure, and complex tasks. Training, making people accountable for their final decision, and tools that show how confident they are can all reduce it (Goddard 2012).

Four questions to ask about any AI tool

Each question goes with one warning sign.

  1. 1

    Whose data did it learn from?

    Do those people and places look like ours? (The data may not fit.)

  2. 2

    What exactly does it predict or produce?

    Is that the thing we really care about? (Wrong target.)

  3. 3

    How do we know its output is right here?

    Has anyone tested it in a setting like ours, and checked that it doesn't make things up? (Makes things up.)

  4. 4

    Who checks the output, and who makes the final call?

    (Over-trust.)

Transcript

Rural health care isn't simply city health care with fewer people. In this section, we'll look at five differences that matter, and what each one means for AI.

By one common count, about 56 million Americans, nearly one in five, live in rural areas. On average, rural communities have higher death rates from trauma, stroke, and cancer than cities do.

First, fewer clinicians and longer trips. Rural areas are short of both family doctors and specialists. And rural Americans live, on average, about ten miles from the nearest hospital, compared with about four miles in cities.

Second, hospitals under pressure. Since 2005, nearly 200 rural hospitals have closed or stopped offering inpatient care. Each closure can mean longer trips in an emergency, and fewer local jobs.

Third, little technical help. A small rural hospital may not have a dedicated IT leader. And rural hospitals rely more heavily than urban ones on the tools that come built into their electronic health record system.

Fourth, weaker digital infrastructure. Many rural areas still lag behind in broadband internet. A tool that needs a fast connection to the cloud can slow down, or stop working, at the worst moment.

Fifth, small numbers. A rural clinic may see only a handful of patients with a given condition. That makes it hard to build a tool from local data, or to check whether a tool works there. In a review of rural AI research, half the studies named a lack of reliable data as a problem.

Rural communities also have real strengths: close relationships, local knowledge, and staff who know their patients well. Good technology should build on those strengths, not replace them.

Put together, a tool that works well in a large city hospital may not fit a small rural clinic. It may need fast internet, technical staff, or data the clinic simply doesn't have.

So before asking whether an AI tool is good, ask whether it fits the place. Next, we'll look at where AI in rural health stands today.

What counts as "rural"?

There is no single definition. Federal agencies draw the line in different places, so rural counts differ:

In the 2020 Census, an urban area has at least 5,000 people or at least 2,000 housing units. Everything else is rural. By this measure, about 66 million people, or 20% of the U.S. population, lived in rural areas in 2020.

Some agencies classify whole counties as metropolitan (metro) or not (nonmetro). About 46 million people, or about 14% of U.S. residents, lived in nonmetro counties in 2024. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Rural-Urban Continuum Codes go further, rating every county from 1 (part of a metro area of 1 million people or more) to 9 (the most rural).

For rural health grants, this office counts all nonmetro counties as rural. It also adds parts of metro counties that are rural in character: census tracts (small areas that usually have a few thousand people) whose residents mostly live in or commute to small towns or rural areas, some very large or rugged tracts, and metro counties with no urban area of 50,000 or more people.

The video's figure, about 56 million people (nearly one in five), comes from a research review that used the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' designation of rural and borderline rural areas (Brown 2026). When you read a rural statistic, check which definition it uses.

Five differences, and what they mean for AI

1. Fewer clinicians, longer trips

What it looks like

Rural areas are short of both family doctors and specialists. Rural Americans live, on average, 10.5 miles from the nearest hospital, compared with 5.6 miles in suburbs and 4.4 miles in cities.

What it means for AI

AI could stretch scarce expertise, for example by screening patients in a local clinic. But fewer staff are available to check its output.

2. Hospitals under pressure

What it looks like

Since January 2005, 197 rural hospitals have closed or stopped offering inpatient care. Closures mean longer trips in an emergency and fewer local jobs.

What it means for AI

Money for new tools is tight. A tool that adds work or cost is unlikely to last, and a tool's errors weigh more where there are fewer backups.

3. Little technical help

What it looks like

A small rural hospital may not have a dedicated IT leader. Among hospitals using AI or other prediction tools, 86% of rural hospitals used tools from their health record vendor, compared with 75% of urban hospitals.

What it means for AI

There is less capacity to test, adjust, and watch tools over time, and more reliance on what the vendor provides.

4. Weaker digital infrastructure

What it looks like

Many rural areas still lag behind in broadband internet.

What it means for AI

A tool that needs a fast cloud connection can slow down or stop working at the worst moment. Plan for what happens when it's offline.

5. Small numbers

What it looks like

A rural clinic may see only a handful of patients with a given condition. In a review of rural AI research, half of the studies (13 of 26) named a lack of reliable data as a problem.

What it means for AI

It is hard to build a tool from local data, or to check whether a tool works locally. Small numbers also make people easier to identify (see Section 4).

Go deeper (optional): security and trust

Security. Ransomware is malicious software that locks an organization's computer systems until a ransom is paid. Ransomware attacks on U.S. hospitals, clinics, and other care providers more than doubled from 2016 to 2021, exposing the personal health information of nearly 42 million patients (Neprash 2022). Every new connected tool is one more system to protect. For a hospital with little technical help, that is a real cost.

Trust. In a 2023 national survey of U.S. adults, about two in three (66%) reported low trust that their health care system would use AI responsibly. More than half (58%) reported low trust that it would make sure an AI tool wouldn't harm them (Nong and Platt 2025). In small communities where people know their clinicians, trust is hard-won and easily lost. Being open about when and how AI is used matters.

Rural strengths

Rural communities also have real strengths: close relationships, local knowledge, and staff who know their patients well. A clinic nurse may know which patients have no car, or who cares for an aging parent. Good technology should build on these strengths, not replace them. A tool that frees staff from paperwork can give them more time for exactly this kind of care.

The takeaway

Before asking whether an AI tool is good, ask whether it fits the place: its staff, internet, money, data, and patients. A tool that works well in a large city hospital may not fit a small rural clinic.

Scenario testing in four steps

Scenario testing means trying a tool on realistic local situations, including messy ones, before anyone relies on it. It is a practical way to answer a question from Section 1: how do we know its output is right here? It builds on what researchers call recurring local validation: testing a tool in each place where it will be used, and checking it again over time (Youssef 2023).

  1. 1

    Pick realistic local cases, including messy ones

    Think about what really happens in your setting: blurry faxes, missing information, unusual names, patients who speak another language.

  2. 2

    Decide what a good result looks like

    Write down the right answer for each case before you run the tool. Decide which mistakes would be minor and which would be dangerous.

  3. 3

    Run the tool quietly first

    In "silent mode," the tool runs and its answers are recorded and compared with the right answers, but no one acts on them yet.

  4. 4

    Keep checking after it goes live

    Forms, patients, and software change. Repeat the checks on a schedule.

A short example

A rural clinic is offered an AI tool that reads faxed referral forms and fills in the scheduling system. Before using it, the clinic tests it on a set of cases: a clean typed form, a handwritten form, a form with a missing date of birth, and a two-page fax with a page out of order. Staff write down the correct answers first. Then they run the tool in silent mode and count its errors. They pay special attention to one question: when information is missing, does the tool say so, or does it make something up?

In a real clinic, this kind of testing uses approved, secure systems and authorized staff. In this tutorial, you'll practice the same idea with made-up forms in Section 5.

Why removing names isn't enough

These rules are written for organizations that handle health data. For you, the rule is simpler: don't put real patient information into a public AI tool at all.

C. Four habits for using any AI tool

Say what you want (the task), give the background (the context), and describe the answer you need (the format). Google's own prompting guide makes the same point.

  • Vague: "Tell me about rural hospitals."
  • Clear: "In five sentences, explain what a critical access hospital is, for a first-year college student."

If the first answer misses, don't start over. Reply with what to change: "Shorter, please, and leave out the history."

AI tools can invent facts, numbers, quotes, and sources. If a reply lists sources, open them and confirm that each one exists and really says what the reply claims. Confirm key facts, numbers, and names with a trusted website, such as a government or university page. If you can't find a source, treat the claim as unverified.

Never paste real patient information, passwords, or confidential files, even with names removed. Before you paste anything, ask: Is it public, or made up? Could it identify a person, even without a name? Would I be comfortable if someone else read it? If you're not sure, don't paste it.

Before relying on a tool for a task, try it on a few realistic examples, including messy ones, and compare its answers with the right ones. That's scenario testing in its simplest form. A tool that does well on tidy examples may stumble on messy ones, and it's better to find out before anyone depends on it.

D. Practice (15 to 20 minutes)

These tasks are set in Wren Creek Family Clinic, a made-up rural clinic. Every name and detail in the practice material is fictional. Start each task in a new Temporary chat, so Gemini can't draw on your earlier chats. (On a school account, where temporary chats aren't available, start a new chat for each task.)

Task 1: Ask clearly (about 4 minutes)

  1. 1
    Send the vague request: "Tell me about critical access hospitals."
  2. 2
    Start another temporary chat. Send the clear request: "In five sentences, explain what a critical access hospital is, for a first-year college student. End with one reason they matter in rural areas."
  3. 3
    Compare the two replies. Which one is easier to use? Which one would fit on a slide?
  4. 4
    Pick one fact from the clear reply, such as the number of beds allowed, and check it on an official page. Good choices: the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services page on critical access hospitals, or the Rural Health Information Hub. (Answer to check against: a critical access hospital has no more than 25 inpatient beds.)

Task 2: Check the facts (about 5 minutes)

  1. 1
    Ask: "How many rural hospitals in the United States have closed or stopped offering inpatient care since 2005? Give the number and list your sources."
  2. 2
    For each source in the reply, ask: Does it exist? Does it give that number? Is it a trusted source, such as a government agency, a university, or a respected research center?
  3. 3
    Compare the reply with the count kept by the Cecil G. Sheps Center for Health Services Research at the University of North Carolina, which tracks rural hospital closures. Its count changes as new closures happen, so note the date you checked.
  4. 4
    Write one sentence on what you found: did the reply match, and were its sources real?

Task 3: Draft and improve (about 5 minutes)

Use only these details, all made up:

  • Event: free community health fair, hosted by Wren Creek Family Clinic
  • Date and time: October 16, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m.
  • Place: Wren Creek Community Center, 210 Main Street
  • Offered: free blood pressure checks, blood sugar checks, flu shots (bring your insurance card if you have one), and a healthy cooking demonstration
  1. 1
    Ask: "Write a friendly announcement of about 100 words for a clinic's community health fair, for a social media post and a printed flyer. Use only these details:" and paste the list.
  2. 2
    Check the draft line by line against the list. Highlight anything that isn't on it, such as a day of the week, a phone number, "no appointment needed," free food, or activities for children. AI tools often add plausible details like these. Even a detail that looks right, such as the weekday, is something you didn't provide, so confirm it before posting.
  3. 3
    Ask Gemini to fix it: "Remove anything that isn't in my list of details." Then ask for one improvement of your choice, such as simpler words or a shorter version.

Two versions of the same made-up referral form came in by fax. Test whether the AI pulls out the same five items from both.

What to notice

Did the score drop on the messy form? Did the tool admit what was missing, or did it fill gaps with guesses? A real clinic would test many more forms before trusting a tool, but the idea is the same.

E. When not to use an AI tool, and how to read along

Don't use a public AI tool for:

  • anything involving real patient information;
  • decisions about someone's health, medicines, or an emergency (call a clinician, or 911 in an emergency);
  • answers you can't check, when being wrong would matter;
  • work your school or employer says must be done without AI.

Here is what the practice tasks typically show. These sample exchanges were written for this tutorial to illustrate common patterns; they are not real Gemini replies, and real replies vary.

The vague request often gets a long, general overview with headings. The clear request gets a short answer at the right level, which you could use almost as is.

A typical reply gives a number and two or three sources. Often one source is real and supports the number, one is real but doesn't mention it, and the date of the count is missing. Checking the Sheps Center page shows the current count and when it was last updated.

A typical draft is friendly and well written, but adds a detail you didn't give, such as a day of the week, "Call 555-0100 to learn more," or "Light refreshments provided." Each one would have to be removed or confirmed before posting.

On Form A, a tool will often score 5 out of 5. On Form B, it may fill in a full first name that isn't on the form, or call the urgency "routine" because no box is checked, for a score of 3 or 4 out of 5. That gap is exactly what scenario testing is meant to find.
