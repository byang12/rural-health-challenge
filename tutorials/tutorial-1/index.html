What makes access different?

Reading & practiceLesson 4 of 34

Two towns, one clinic each

These are invented comparisons to illustrate the question, not descriptions of real places.

Community A

The clinic offers appointments most weekdays and coordinates rides with a community group. A needed specialty service is 20 miles away.

Community B

The clinic opens fewer days and the nearest specialty service is farther away. Residents may depend on relatives or a limited transport service.

Which clues would you collect?

Think about what a clinic map reveals—and what it hides.

What care is actually offered? How often? What are the hours? Facility counts alone cannot answer.

How do residents travel? Does the schedule work with appointment times? The same distance may be easy for one person and impossible for another.

Which local organizations, family networks, or transportation programs help people obtain care?

What do residents say about costs, trust, language, time away from work, and receiving information? Never infer their experiences from a county average.

A building does not equal access

The key idea

The presence of a clinic tells us a facility exists. It does not show which services it offers or whether residents can use them. Good data collection begins by asking what else we need to know.

From Problems to Questions

Investigate the referral clues

Reading & practiceLesson 11 of 34

A fictional appointment story

The scenario is constructed for teaching. It is not a real patient story.

What the records show

Cedar Bridge Clinic sent a specialty referral. Its receipt was acknowledged. An appointment was booked.

What they do not show

The supplied excerpt does not say whether the appointment was completed, canceled, or rescheduled.

What we cannot assume

We cannot conclude that the patient missed care, that staff made an error, or that transportation caused the outcome.

Three types of questions

Did the scheduled visit happen? A permitted visit-status record could address that fact.

How does the rural clinic check an unconfirmed outcome? Staff workflow guidance or an authorized conversation could help.

What travel options exist for specialty appointments? Public transport information and community input could help frame the issue—but could not establish this person's experience.

Permitted scheduling records, clinic hours, and available services help explain what care was offered or documented.

Explore two public resources

The American Community Survey describes populations and households, including transportation and internet access. It relies on survey estimates for specific periods.

PLACES, from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, provides local estimates of health measures. Its geographic and statistical coverage must be checked.

The Health Resources and Services Administration offers information on certain federally supported health centers; it is not a complete specialist directory.

Reading & practiceLesson 18 of 34

Same setting, different questions

Question A: Is a visit completed?

Choose a permitted scheduling or visit-status record. Public county statistics cannot establish the answer.

Question B: Could lack of vehicles affect access?

Start with Census household-vehicle estimates, then consider transport services and local perspectives. A county estimate does not establish an individual cause.

Question C: How might air quality relate to respiratory health?

An EPA air-monitoring source may provide environmental context. You still need an appropriate health measure and careful interpretation.

What sources may overlook

Small populations

Estimates can be uncertain or unavailable, and privacy protections may limit detail.

Different geographic levels

A county average may hide variation between neighborhoods and households.

Local experience

Community conversations may reveal barriers or strengths that national datasets cannot capture. They require thoughtful questions and appropriate permissions.

Reading & practiceLesson 22 of 34

Could digital access affect healthcare options?

Start with a rural health question, not a data download.

Our question

What can county household internet information contribute to understanding conditions for telehealth access?

What we need

An official measure of households with a broadband Internet subscription, for a stated county and period.

What it cannot answer

Whether any specific patient has a usable device, stable service, digital skills, privacy, or a telehealth appointment.

What is Census QuickFacts?

The publisher

The U.S. Census Bureau provides publicly accessible facts about states and counties.

The measure

The QuickFacts section Computer and Internet Use includes household broadband-subscription information.

Our location

We will examine Hale County, Alabama, as public geographic context, not as a real patient or clinic case.

Where and How Do We Find Data?

Check the source suggested by AI

Reading & practiceLesson 23 of 34

The instructor's 20-second AI example

You do not need to open an AI account. The demonstration is shown in the video.

Prompt shown in the video

For a question about internet access and telehealth in rural Alabama, suggest one official public source, one useful indicator, and the publisher link. Do not invent numerical values. Identify what must be checked.

Important distinction

The AI response is a lead to verify. It is not the original dataset and cannot establish a percentage or a patient's outcome.

Verify the original Census page

If you cannot access the live site, use the verified, dated screenshot supplied with the tutorial. No numeric value is required for the practice unless the original source actually shows it.

01

Open the official Hale County, Alabama, QuickFacts profile.

02

Confirm the site belongs to the U.S. Census Bureau.

03

Find the Computer and Internet Use section.

04

Read the measure named Households with a broadband Internet subscription, percent.

05

Check that the selected area is Hale County, Alabama, and copy the reference period actually displayed.

06

If an AI-generated value, date, or claim does not match the publisher, do not use it.

How to interpret the indicator

What this measure describes

A share of county households reporting a broadband internet subscription during the listed period.

What this measure does not prove

That every person can use telehealth, that a named clinic provides telehealth, or that a fictional patient had an appointment.

Why the time period matters

Public indicators have reference periods. Always use the period displayed on the original page, not a date an AI assistant guesses.

Separate facts from assumptions

Reading & practiceLesson 28 of 34

The referral example: What can we say?

Supported

The fictional excerpt documents a referral, acknowledgment, and booking.

Not established

The excerpt does not show whether the visit happened.

Next permitted check

An authorized status record or approved explanation of how follow-up is documented.

Where AI fits — optional

You do not need an AI account to complete this tutorial.

Useful assistance

An AI tool can help rewrite a public source description in plain language or organize a short evidence note.

What AI cannot do

It cannot create a missing visit outcome or turn a modeled county estimate into a patient record.

How to check an AI summary

Compare every important measure, year, place, and claim with the original public source. Reject anything the source does not support.

Can We Trust What We Found?

Find the difference between data and a conclusion

Reading & practiceLesson 29 of 34

Two claims, two levels of evidence

Supported

'The fictional excerpt documents a referral and a booking.' This matches the supplied teaching clues.

Unsupported

'Transportation caused the missed appointment.' Neither a missed visit nor its cause was documented.

Good next step

'With appropriate permission, check the completion status and how follow-up is recorded.' This identifies evidence without inventing an answer.

Three traps in interpreting data

A blank does not mean zero

A missing status can reflect incomplete documentation. It is not automatically evidence that nothing happened.

A county estimate does not describe everyone

Geographic averages and modeled population estimates cannot be used as personal medical records.

Confident AI text is not independent evidence

Treat an AI summary as a draft explanation. Verify numbers, dates, populations, and sources before using it.
