The Challenge

About the Challenge

The question, purpose, objectives, and scope of the 2026–2027 student challenge.

The challenge question

What is one practical change that could help a rural healthcare provider serve its community more sustainably, and what evidence supports that recommendation?

Purpose

The IRHR–ALAAI Rural Health Transformation Challenge connects interdisciplinary student learning with the priorities of rural healthcare providers and communities. It focuses on the relationship between dependable services, operating resources, staff capacity, and community access.

Teams investigate a manageable question using evidence and explicit assumptions. Their work gives local partners something concrete to review: a proposed change, a supporting comparison, and the conditions under which the recommendation could work.

Overall objective

Equip interdisciplinary student teams to use community evidence, data analysis, business reasoning, and AI tools to develop and justify one feasible improvement to rural healthcare sustainability and access.

What participants will learn

  1. Define a locally relevant problem. Describe one challenge informed by provider and community priorities, identify who is affected, and explain the operating constraints.
  2. Build and assess the evidence. Select, organize, and check relevant data; document sources; and distinguish observations, estimates, assumptions, and information gaps.
  3. Evaluate one feasible improvement. Compare current practice with a proposed change and assess its relevant costs, staffing needs, operating effects, and community benefits.
  4. Use AI with informed judgment. Apply AI to a defined task, verify important outputs, and explain its value, limitations, and need for human review.
  5. Collaborate and communicate. Combine perspectives across disciplines and explain a recommendation, its tradeoffs, and a practical next validation step.

Possible project questions

FocusExample question
Appointment utilizationCould a change in appointment hours better match demand within existing staffing?
Referral coordinationCould an administrative completeness check reduce avoidable referral rework?
Staff workloadCould a clearer division of routine tasks free time for follow-up?
Transportation and accessCould a different arrangement for transport or service timing improve access?
Shared resourcesCould two providers share a limited administrative or coordination function?
Information qualityCould a more reliable service directory reduce unsuccessful calls and referrals?

Each team chooses one question with the organizers. The proposal should remain narrow enough to analyze using the available time and evidence.

How the program works

Organizers provide a case brief, starting data, practical templates, common assumptions, and shared training. Teams can ask mentors for clarification and receive feedback on scope and reasoning. Additional interviews or local data collection are optional and coordinated through the program.

Financial effects, workload changes, and community benefits should be explained separately. A modeled improvement is a reason to investigate further; its assumptions must remain visible.

Research that informs the design

The program draws on rural community-oriented student projects, interdisciplinary health innovation events, and research on evaluating clinic operations. These sources support community involvement, mentoring, focused comparisons, and assessment of the work students produce. The first cohort will help organizers assess the feasibility and usefulness of this particular format. See References R1–R8 for the design foundations.
