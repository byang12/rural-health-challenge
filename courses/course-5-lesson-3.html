Course 5 · Lesson 3 of 5

Keep a person in the loop

Reading time: about 5 minutes

In this lesson

Explain what the AI sped up, where it slipped, and what a person still had to do.

You will leave with three short notes for your team that together make an honest account of AI’s role in your project.

Key terms
Person in the loop
A person checks every AI answer, fixes what is wrong, and follows up, before anything reaches a patient or a partner.
Automation bias
The tendency to over-rely on automated advice, even when it is wrong.

Make sense of your results

After Lesson 2 you have your own results. This lesson is about making sense of them, because one finding shows up again and again: AI makes the first pass faster, but it does not remove the person.

What went quickly

On your scoring sheet, compare the minutes until the AI’s first answer with your minutes for P02 by hand. For a person, that first read is slow and tiring, especially at the end of a long day, so a fast first pass is real help, as long as it is right.

Where it slipped

The practice packets include a few traps that can catch people and AI tools alike: a visit note that looks current but is too old, a patient’s name spelled differently on the insurance card, and insurance plans with different rules about prior authorization. Each one needs judgment: comparing two dates against a 90-day rule, noticing that two names do not quite match, and applying the right plan’s rule to the right specialty. AI tools can get these wrong while sounding completely sure. Your own run may have slipped there, somewhere else, or not at all. What matters is that you checked.

Show the three traps in the practice packets (open after your Lesson 2 runs)
  • P03: the visit note is from April, but the referral is from September, more than 90 days apart.
  • P08: the insurance card says Johnathon; the referral form says Jonathan.
  • P05: the patient has State Medicaid, which needs prior authorization only for orthopedics, so none is needed for this cardiology referral.

Confidence is a trap for the person too

People tend to over-rely on automated advice, a pattern researchers call automation bias. It grows with heavy workload, time pressure, and trust in the system, and training and clear accountability help reduce it (Goddard et al., 2012). In practice, the checker looks at the packet, not just at the AI’s list. If the AI says “nothing missing,” check anyway.

A person stays in the loop

Someone checks every answer, fixes what is wrong (for example, by calling for a missing signature or requesting a test report), and follows up when a specialist’s office sends a packet back. That work takes real time, and it has to be counted. This is why the time saved is smaller than the AI’s speed suggests: the AI shortens one step, but the checking, fixing, and follow-up remain.

An honest result can be “AI adds little”

If checking the AI’s answers takes as long as doing the job by hand, say so. For a clinic deciding where to spend its time, that is a useful finding, not a failure.

Limits to keep in mind

The traps were built into the practice packets on purpose, so you are likely to meet them. Real packets bring their own problems, and Lesson 4 looks at what happens when the work gets messier.

Use it in your project

Try it: three notes for your team

On the reflection sheet, write down three things: what the AI sped up, where it slipped, and what a person still had to do. Together they describe AI’s role and limits for your proposal, and the human review your proposal includes.

Check your understanding

1. The AI’s answer for a packet says “nothing missing.” What should staff do?

  1. Send the packet; the AI has already checked it.
  2. Check the packet against the checklist anyway, especially dates, names, and plan rules.
  3. Run the AI again until two answers agree.
Show the answer

Answer: B. A confident answer can still be wrong, and the AI’s slips tend to be on items that need judgment. The checker looks at the packet, not just at the AI’s list.

Sending a packet on the AI’s word alone is automation bias at work (A). Two matching answers can both be wrong (C). A person stays in the loop.

2. Suppose the AI gives its first answer for a packet in seconds. Why can the time saved per packet still be small?

  1. The first answer is only part of the job: checking, fixing, and follow-up still take a person’s time, and that time counts.
  2. Fast answers are usually wrong, so they should be ignored.
  3. The time saved is only small when the clinic has few packets.
Show the answer

Answer: A. The AI shortens one step, the first read. Checking every answer, fixing what is wrong, and following up stay with a person, so the saving is smaller than the AI’s speed alone suggests.

Speed says nothing about whether an answer is right, so fast answers need checking, not ignoring (B). The time saved per packet does not depend on how many packets there are (C). Count the whole job, with a person in the loop.
