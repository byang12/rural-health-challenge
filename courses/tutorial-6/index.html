What this tutorial is about

Real Alabama numbers, and the five parts ahead.

Read the video text

Imagine that you have a good idea for a small clinic in rural Alabama. It could save staff time, or help patients get the care they need. But an idea only helps if the people who decide understand it, trust it, and can act on it. This tutorial is about that last step.

Rural health care in Alabama is hard work. More than two million Alabamians live in rural areas. Since 2005, nine rural hospitals in the state have closed or stopped overnight care. In 1980, hospitals in forty-five rural counties delivered babies. On the state’s 2025 map, only fifteen did. Many clinics have few staff and small budgets. That is why a good recommendation matters.

This tutorial has five short parts. First, you will see why a rural recommendation is different. Second, you will learn who needs to hear it. Third, you will check how far you can trust your evidence. Fourth, you will write a recommendation that a provider can act on. Fifth, you will see how AI can help, and where people must decide.

You do not need a technical background. Each part has a short video, a few lines to read, and quick questions. There are no grades. Go at your own speed, and repeat anything you want. Let us begin with a simple question. What makes a rural recommendation different?

One Monday at a rural clinic

A made-up clinic manager receives a 20-page report.

Read the video text

Meet a clinic manager in a small town in rural Alabama. This manager is made up, but the day you will see is typical. On Monday morning, a student team sends a twenty-page report with a good idea. Let us see what happens to it.

At eight o’clock, the manager is already doing three jobs: the schedule, a billing question, and the front desk, because someone is sick. There is no time to read twenty pages. If the main point is not in the first few lines, it may never be read. That is the first thing to remember. Few staff, and little time.

Suppose the manager does read it. The idea needs one more staff member. But the budget is very small. An idea the clinic cannot pay for is not a real option. Second: small budgets.

The idea also changes how referrals work. A referral is when a patient is sent to another provider, such as a specialist. This clinic sends referrals by fax to a hospital in another county. So the clinic cannot make the change alone. The hospital must agree too. Third: the work crosses organizations.

Next, the manager remembers the last program that came to town. A team from a city collected information, made promises, and left. Nothing changed. So people here are careful with outsiders. Fourth: trust takes time.

And one more thing. In a small town, people know each other. A person seen at a mental health visit may be talked about. So be careful with private information.

These are some of the reasons rural health is so hard. They also make it important. Now read a few lines, and try two quick questions.

A recommendation asks people to change their work. Rural towns have strengths, like trusted local people and groups. But four things make change harder.

Few staff.

One person may do three jobs, with little time to read or train.

Small budgets.

Even a small new cost may be impossible.

Work crosses organizations.

A referral may go by fax to a hospital in another county.

Trust takes time.

People remember programs that came and left.

One finding, four people

The same made-up finding told to four different people.

Read the video text

A student team has one finding. At a small clinic, nobody tracks a referral after it is sent. Some patients never see the specialist. This example is made up. Let us share this one finding with four different people.

First, the clinic manager. The manager decides. The manager asks: what does it cost, and what could go wrong? So the team says: “We suggest a two-month test. One staff member keeps a simple list of open referrals and checks it twice a week. We would count how many are still open after fourteen days.”

Second, the staff who do the work. They ask: how will my week change? So the team says: “You would keep one list and check it twice a week. If a referral is open after fourteen days, you call the specialist’s office.”

Third, the specialist’s office, at the other end of the referral. They ask: what do you need from us? So the team says: “Please tell us when you get our referral, and when the visit is scheduled. After the visit, please send us your report.” Getting a referral is not the same as seeing the patient. So the clinic keeps checking until the report comes back.

Fourth, the patient. The patient asks: what should I do? So the message is simple. “If you have not heard about your specialist visit in one week, please call us.”

The finding stays the same. The words change, because each person has a different question. The times in these messages are made up for teaching. A real clinic sets them by how urgent the visit is, and by its own rules. And one more thing. Listen before you write. Ask staff and patients what they need, and what already works, before you decide what to recommend.

Three kinds of evidence

Real public data and a real Alabama program, shown on their own websites.

Read the video text

How far can you trust your evidence? Let us look at three kinds, using real examples from Alabama.

First, public data. This is CDC PLACES, a free source of health estimates for every county. CDC is the national public health agency. It estimates that twenty-seven percent of adults in Greene County have diagnosed diabetes. In Tuscaloosa County, it is twelve percent. This shows a difference between counties. But this data describes all adults in a county. It does not describe the patients of one clinic.

Second, programs from other places. This is the Rural Health Information Hub, or RHIhub. Its Models and Innovations section lists rural programs by topic. Under Transportation, we find Kid One Transport, a real program in Alabama. It gives rides to medical visits for children and pregnant women. Its page shares results that families reported. Many reported fewer emergency room visits. That is useful. But families reported this themselves, and the page does not describe a comparison group. So we say “families reported.” We do not say “the rides caused it.”

Before you use a program, ask three questions. Was it tried in a rural place? Did it need money or staff that your clinic does not have? Did it serve people like yours? Kid One uses about twenty vehicles and trained drivers. A small clinic could not run this alone, but it could work with a program like it.

Third, local evidence. A manager tells you, “We follow up on every referral.” That is useful, but it is only what someone said. Ask staff to show you the steps with a blank form or a made-up case. Do not ask to see real patient records. Seeing the work is stronger than hearing about it. But one example does not show that it always happens. A document helps too, but a written rule does not prove that people follow it.

So say how you know. Now read a few lines, and try the questions.

Use three kinds of evidence, and know the limits of each.

Public data

describes a whole group, such as all adults in a county. It does not describe one clinic’s patients.

Programs from other places

show what happened there. Ask if it would fit here.

Local evidence

comes from staff and patients. Say how you know: someone said it, you saw it, or a document shows it.

Words to know
Evidence
Information that supports what you say.
Estimate
A careful calculation. It is not an exact count.
Adjusted for age
Changed so that places with older or younger people can be compared fairly.
Comparison group
A similar group without the program. It shows what might have happened anyway.

From weak to useful

A weak recommendation is fixed in four steps, then checked with a listener.

Read the video text

Here is a recommendation a student team might write. It is made up, and it is weak. “Rural clinics face many challenges, and new technology is changing health care. Our solution will save money and help patients get care faster. The clinic should adopt it.” Let us fix it in four steps.

Step one. Start with the decision. Put the request in the first sentence. So we write: “We suggest that the clinic test one change for two months. One staff member keeps a list of open referrals and checks it twice a week.” Then say why you chose this change. “We also thought about a new computer system, but it would cost too much for a first test.”

Step two. Say what you know, and what you do not know. “Staff told us that referrals get lost, but we have not measured how often.” Honesty builds trust.

Step three. Show the cost. The weak version said it would save money. But this change adds staff time. So we write: “This adds about ten minutes of staff time, twice a week. We do not know yet whether it saves money.” Also ask what could get worse. Does the work just move to someone else?

Step four. Give a small next step. Who does it, by when, and how will you know it worked? “First, staff look at last month’s referrals: out of every ten, how many were still open after fourteen days? Each month, the manager checks again. If fewer stay open, and staff have the time, the clinic continues. If not, it changes the plan or stops.” Also ask who will keep it going after that.

Now check your words. Read it to someone outside your field. Ask them to explain it back. A listener might say: “So one person checks a list of referrals twice a week, for two months, and then you decide if it helped?” If they cannot explain it in their own words, change it.

Planning a test? Check six things
  1. What do you want to improve?
  2. What happens now, before the change?
  3. What will you measure during the test?
  4. Who will do the work, and for how long?
  5. What staff time or cost will it take, and what could get worse?
  6. What result would make you continue, change, or stop?

Useful, but check it

A made-up example of AI making a sentence clearer, and adding a false number.

Read the video text

AI tools can help you write a recommendation. They can also cause problems. Let us see how to use them well.

AI is good at three jobs. It can turn rough notes into a first draft. It can rewrite hard text in plain English. And it can act as a tough reader, and ask you hard questions.

Here is a hard sentence. “Interoperability constraints impede closed-loop referral tracking.” We ask AI to rewrite it for a busy clinic manager. Now it says: “Our computer systems do not connect, so we cannot easily see if a referral was completed.” That is much clearer. But look at the next sentence. “This will save the clinic forty thousand dollars a year.” We never said that. We have no source for it. AI can invent facts that sound true. This example is made up, but this problem is real.

So follow three rules. One. Check every number and every source. If you cannot find a source, remove it. Two. Protect private information. Never paste patient information, or a clinic’s computer security details, into a public AI tool. Three. Say how you used AI, and how you checked it.

AI can draft, organize, and compare. But people decide. AI should not make medical decisions, and it should not choose for a provider. You are responsible for every sentence you use. Now read a few lines, and try the questions. You can also copy three useful prompts.

You have reached the end of the tutorial series. Here is one last idea. A recommendation is the start of a conversation with a community. Rural clinics in Alabama have heard many promises. They remember the people who listened, told the truth about what they knew, and suggested a first step small enough to try.
