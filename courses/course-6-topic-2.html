The 5–7 slide presentation blueprint

Reading time: about 4 minutes

In this topic

Structure a 5–7 slide deck and divide an eight-minute presentation across your three-person team.

You will leave with a six-slide storyboard with speaking roles and time limits.

Your presentation is your proposal, spoken

Judges will hear your presentation and read your proposal, so the two must match: the same recommendation, the same numbers, and visuals taken from your supporting artifact. Do not write a new story for the slides. Build the deck from the proposal sections you outlined in Topic 1, using the presentation outline.

Eight minutes is short. Six slides give each slide a little over a minute: enough for one clear point per slide, and no room for a long introduction.

Six slides, three speakers

Split the eight minutes so every teammate speaks and each part has a clear job.

Speaker 1 · about 2½ minutes

  • Slide 1, the hook and decision. Introduce the team, the clinic’s challenge question, and your three-sentence recommendation. Say the recommendation now, not at the end.
  • Slide 2, clinic context and evidence. Who is affected at the clinic, and the key inputs you used, each labeled observed, estimated, or assumed.

Speaker 2 · about 3 minutes

  • Slide 3, the proposed solution. A simple workflow diagram showing current practice next to the proposed change.
  • Slide 4, why it is solid. The main comparison table or chart from your supporting artifact: baseline against the proposed change.
  • Slide 5, impact on the rural clinic. Three columns: staff capacity, cost and resources, and community access.

Speaker 3 · about 2½ minutes

  • Slide 6, limitations and next step. The key assumptions, how AI outputs were checked, and the practical next validation step for the clinic.

Speaker 3 has one slide but carries the part judges probe most: what could go wrong and what to test first. Speaker 3 can also open the question period and pass each question to the teammate who leads on it (Topic 4).

Five or seven slides?

Six slides is a guide, not a rule. Merge slides 1 and 2 if your context is simple, or add a seventh slide if your workflow needs a second diagram. Keep the same order and the same eight minutes.

Practice case: a storyboard

Each headline is a full sentence that states the slide’s point. Read the six headlines in order and you hear the whole argument.

Six-slide storyboard for the practice case
SlideHeadlineWhat the audience sees
1We recommend a one-week timing check of an assisted referral workflowTeam names; the clinic’s question; the three-sentence recommendation
2Twenty referral packets a week take about 200 staff minutesWho prepares packets today; the five key inputs, each labeled assumed
3Staff review every assisted packet before it is sentTwo-lane diagram: current steps and proposed steps
4Under the teaching assumptions, assisted handling uses 140 instead of 200 minutes a weekThe comparison table from the supporting artifact
5One staff hour freed, $20 a week in new cash cost, and an access benefit not yet testedThree columns: staff capacity, cost, community access
6Above about 8 minutes per packet, the freed time no longer covers the subscriptionWeekly minutes at 7, 8, and 10 minutes per packet; the AI check; the next step and its decision rule

Time your words

Most people speak about 110–130 words per minute when presenting. That is about 275–325 words for a 2½-minute part and 330–390 words for a 3-minute part.

Write speaker notes, not a script to read aloud. Rehearse with a timer and aim to finish in about 7½ minutes, which leaves room for a slow start or a handoff. Practice the two handoffs so the talk sounds like one argument rather than three separate talks.

Practice activity

Try it: storyboard your deck

Make a six-row storyboard: slide number, a headline that states the point, what the audience sees, the speaker, and the time. Read the headlines aloud in order. If they tell your whole argument without the slides, the structure works.

Check your storyboard

  • Does slide 1 state the recommendation, not just the topic?
  • Does each slide come from a proposal section, with the same numbers?
  • Does each speaker have a time limit, and do the limits add up to under eight minutes?
  • Does the final slide name the weakest assumption and the next step?
