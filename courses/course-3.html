Course 3 of 6

Rural Healthcare Sustainability Analysis

Four stages for judging whether a change can last: understand the problem, establish the baseline, compare options, and test your recommendation. One realistic referral case runs through all four.

Preparation lead: Shenglin Li

Learning goal

Use AI to develop a sustainability analysis whose evidence, calculations, assumptions, and conclusions can be checked.

First edition

This first edition contains the four stage readings, each with two self-check questions, and the case workbook. It is based on the one-hour workshop plan. Your instructor shares the reference solutions after you submit your worksheet.

What sustainability means here

In this course, rural healthcare sustainability is the ability to keep needed services running and adapt them, with enough funding, staff, and operating capacity, while keeping quality and community access acceptable. This is a working definition for the course, informed by research on program sustainability (S5).

Funding concerns the money needed to keep a service operating, now and after startup support ends.

Staff and operating capacity concerns how time and effort are used, and who keeps the work going.

Quality and community access concerns whether people can still obtain an appropriate service.

A change may help one of these without showing any benefit in the others. Freed staff time may make room for follow-up, but it does not automatically lower payroll. Faster paperwork does not by itself prove that patients reach the specialist sooner. State the link you are assuming and the evidence that would check it.

The main tool in this course is sensitivity analysis: changing an uncertain input and checking how the result changes. It shows what your conclusion depends on. It does not prove that the changed value will happen.

What this course covers

Course 3 turns your question into a comparison that someone else can check. You decide what the clinic is choosing, measure how the work is done now, compare realistic options, and test whether your answer survives when an uncertain number changes. No statistics or programming background is needed: the calculations are sums, averages, and simple rates.

The practice case: Teaching Clinic A

Teaching Clinic A is a fictional rural clinic whose coordinator prepares specialist referrals alongside other office work. The manager wants to reduce avoidable paperwork without removing patient support or necessary checks. Using 20 invented referral records, your pair compares three options: current practice, a standard packet checklist, and AI-assisted packet preparation with a person checking every draft. Recommending the checklist can be just as strong an answer as recommending AI.

Real problem, invented numbers

The referral problem and the workflow sources are real. Every record, time, cost, and limit in the case is invented for teaching and was not measured at any named organization. The records contain no real patient information.

Two ways AI appears in this course

As your analysis assistant

AI organizes the evidence you supply, proposes formulas, compares scenarios, and questions your conclusion. You check its work.

As a possible service change

In the case, AI drafts a referral cover sheet and flags missing fields. A trained person checks every draft. It cannot invent clinical content, decide urgency, or send a referral by itself.

How the course feeds your project

  • Proposal: the problem statement, the baseline, and the comparison of options (Course 6 shows where each goes).
  • Supporting analysis: your calculation table, with formulas, labels, and the review-time scenarios.
  • Presentation: what would change your recommendation, and what still needs to be measured.

Treat any analysis like this one as preliminary. Before a real clinic adopts a change, it needs local evidence: a small pilot that records review and correction effort, checks that required information is preserved, and follows patient outcomes. It also needs clear answers about who pays, who maintains the process, and who covers the work when staff are absent.

Check your work

  • Does every comparison use the same referrals and the same time period?
  • Is every input and claim labeled, and can a reader trace it to a row or a source?
  • Did you count human review, setup, and maintenance, and keep cash separate from staff time?
  • Is your recommendation conditional, with an owner and an outcome still to be measured?
Suggested reading

The workshop needs only the case reading in Topic 5. For background: Harder et al. (R6) and Ortiz and Wan (R10). For a practical example of distinct workflow and financial outcomes, see Shah et al. (R7).
