Course 4 · Topic 2 of 5

Map the workflow and staff capacity

Reading time: about 5 minutes

In this topic

Map current and proposed referral steps by role, including review and correction, and calculate the weekly staff time the change releases.

You will leave with a timed role map for your supporting artifact.

Key terms
Role-lane map
A diagram with one row (a “lane”) for each person or team. Each step sits in the lane of whoever does it.
Handoff
A point where work passes from one person to another. Delays and errors often happen here.
Released time
Staff time the change frees up. It is potential capacity, not a guaranteed saving.

How to map a workflow

A role-lane map shows who does each step

List the steps of the process in order and place each step in the lane of the person or team who does it. Arrows show the order. An arrow that crosses from one lane to another marks a handoff, where waiting and errors often occur. Exceptions, such as a packet returned for correction, appear as their own steps. Use the same symbols in every map, so a reader can compare the before and after versions directly.

Times must cover the same work before and after

To compare current practice with a proposed change, attach a time to each step and add up the times for the same set of tasks. If the new process includes a review step, the review time belongs in the new total. Leaving it out makes the change look better than it is, as Course 3 showed with an extra 3 minutes of review per packet.

Work often moves rather than disappears. Kamalahmadi et al. (2026) found that telehealth let physicians care for more patients but added follow-up work through patient messages.

Practice case: four lanes and five handoffs

In the shared case, the lanes are front desk, coordinator, nurse, and partner. Every arrow between lanes is a handoff, and this map has five of them.

Every arrow crosses between lanes, so each one is a handoff. Draw the current process first: clinic staff can check a map of work they already do before they judge a proposed one.

Time released—60 minutes, or 1 staff hour per week

In the proposal, this hour goes to follow-up and transport calls, which ties the time released to a task the clinic needs.

Limits to keep in mind

Released time is potential capacity

Sixty minutes released is a modeled result on invented inputs. It does not show that referrals finish sooner or that patients reach their visits.

Harder work can also move somewhere else. Tushe et al. (2025) studied a clinic that added a remote (message-based) consultation channel. Average waits for in-person referrals fell by 37.5%, but recommended in-person visit time rose by 20% and required clinical resources by 8.5%, because more complex patients moved to in-person visits. A complete map therefore follows each case into the partner lane and asks where the harder work went.

If you replace teaching times with real ones, Gale et al. (2022), the chartbook in the course’s suggested reading, describes how rural health clinics can compare staffing and productivity with national data. Those historical figures still need local checking.

Use it in your project

Try it: draw your timed role map

Draw the role-lane map for your process, before and after the change, for the same volume and the same week. Write the time beside each step, including review and correction. Put the map in your supporting artifact and copy the weekly staff-time totals into your practice table. Topic 3 then attaches costs to the resources the map shows.

Check your understanding

1. Assisted drafting takes 5 minutes and nurse review adds 2 minutes per packet. Manual handling takes 10 minutes. At 20 packets a week, how much staff time does the change release?

  1. 100 minutes per week.
  2. 60 minutes per week.
  3. 40 minutes per week.
  4. 140 minutes per week.
Show the answer

Answer: B. 20 × (10 − (5 + 2)) = 60 minutes, or one staff hour. A time comparison must count every step that remains, including review.

A counts drafting only and leaves out review. C is the time released if the assisted total were 8 minutes. D is the assisted workload, not the time released. Subtract the full assisted time from the manual time, then multiply by the number of packets.

2. After a new referral channel starts, waits for specialist referrals fall. What should the workflow map also check?

  1. Nothing; shorter waits prove the change works.
  2. Whether harder cases or extra work moved to another step or team.
  3. Only the software price.
  4. Whether the front desk likes the tool.
Show the answer

Answer: B. A map is complete only when it shows where the work goes. In the Tushe et al. study, shorter waits came with longer in-person visits for more complex patients.

A stops at one good result. C and D look at cost or opinion, not at the workflow. Follow each case through every lane, including the partner lane, before counting time as released.
