Data Curation and Integration

Rural healthcare decisions often depend on information collected by different people, systems, and organizations. Before those sources can support a useful comparison, their definitions, time periods, and record structures need to agree.

Preparation lead: Xiaoming Guo

This course introduces a practical approach to organizing, checking, and combining data for sustainability analysis and service planning. Using a fictional appointment case, you will learn how to make each result traceable to its source and explain what the prepared data can support.

What you will learn

Course goal

Prepare a consistent, traceable dataset that another team member can understand and use for a rural healthcare question.

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  • Define the evidence: identify what each record represents, the population covered, the reporting period, and the meaning of important fields.
  • Integrate with justification: reconcile source-specific codes, reconstruct comparable records, and preserve missing values and source information.
  • Check and communicate the result: reproduce a small comparison, explain its limitations, and prepare a documented handoff for analysis.

A practical example

Two sites of a fictional rural provider want to understand missed appointments before choosing a follow-up intervention. One system supplies one record per appointment; the other records several status changes for the same appointment. Even a familiar code can be misleading: C means “completed” in one system and “canceled” in the other.

You will see why combining these exports requires more than matching column names. After preparing comparable appointment records, the example explores how differences in the types of services each clinic provides can affect an overall comparison. The exercise connects data preparation to a practical question: what evidence is ready for analysis, and what still needs investigation?

Fictional teaching data

All case records and dictionaries are fictional teaching material.

Topics and schedule

The course has five short topics. Each combines a reading, a guided example, and an understanding check. Times are estimates for guided study with prepared data. The optional lab provides additional hands-on practice.

Methods you will use

Data documentation

The Data Documentation Initiative (DDI) provides concepts for describing what a variable means, how its values are represented, and where it is used. We apply these ideas through a small data dictionary and clear processing rules.

Traceability and quality

Preserve original sources, record transformations, and distinguish unknown values from zero. Checks cover keys, definitions, time periods, exclusions, and reproducible calculations.

Human review of AI assistance

AI may propose a mapping, but its source evidence and reasoning still need review. The course can also be completed entirely through manual inspection.

What you will produce

For your team project, Course 2 supports three parts of your existing analysis:

  • A curated dataset with consistent records and retained source references.
  • A data dictionary explaining fields, units, codes, and missing-value meanings.
  • A quality-check record documenting important decisions, checks, and remaining limitations.

During the guided core, you review the prepared example and record your decisions. The optional lab lets you practice the workflow in more detail; applying it to your team’s own sources takes additional project time.

Course 1 establishes the collection question and sources; Course 3 uses the prepared evidence to investigate operating pressures. The same work supports your final proposal and presentation.

Course resources

Learning materials. During the course, participants will use a worked appointment example and reusable templates for data definitions, source records, mapping decisions, quality checks, and handoff notes. These materials will be shared with participants as part of course delivery.

Further reading and project resources:

  • Optional DDI-CDI reference: background on describing data meaning, structure, and provenance.
  • Program resources: public-data starting points and materials for the wider challenge.
