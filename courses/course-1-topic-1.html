Course 1 · Topic 1 of 6

Define the task and data requirements

Reading time: about 5 minutes

In this topic

Turn one assigned referral problem into a bounded AI collection task, with a clear record unit and the fields you need.

For your project: you start the capture plan, which describes the small dataset that Course 2 will receive.

Key terms
Referral handoff
Passing a patient’s referral, and the information that goes with it, from one organization to another. Here, from a rural clinic to an urban specialty service.
Bounded AI task
An instruction with clear limits: which sources the AI may use, what it should return, and what it must not do.
Record unit
What one row of your table represents. For example, one event written in a log, not one patient.
Field
One column of the table: a piece of information collected for every row.
Synthetic
Invented for teaching. Synthetic records look realistic but describe no real person or clinic.

Why learn data collection when AI can do it?

In this course, you will use AI to do part of the collection work. Your job is to specify a useful task, give the AI suitable materials, and deliver information that another learner can understand and check. You are not asked to copy everything by hand or to build a referral-management application. When an AI extraction is correct, accept it without rewriting it. The important question is whether the result answers the assigned task.

The setting: a real problem, invented records

The course uses an imagined referral handoff from a rural clinic to an urban health system. It is based on a published study by Sutherland and colleagues, who interviewed 44 people involved in this kind of care. They described referrals that took a long time, difficult contacts between providers, and electronic health record systems (the clinics’ computer records) that did not connect with each other [1].

What the study does and does not provide

The study gives a good reason to look at referral coordination. It does not provide the classroom records, and it does not measure any AI tool. The three problems in this course are teaching adaptations, not the authors’ own findings.

Three problems, three kinds of record

Each problem produces a different kind of row. That difference decides what the AI should return, and what Course 2 can later combine.

The three referral problems and what one row represents in each
ProblemQuestionOne row represents
1. Information is incomplete or scatteredWhat administrative information is in the supplied referral packets?One packet checked against one checklist item
2. Progress is hard to reconstructWhich events and dates show how a referral moved forward?One event statement written in a source
3. Follow-up responsibility is unclearWhat do different staff roles say about who follows up?One person’s account of one step in the work

All three problems are demonstrated, but your own short-course work follows one of them. The source cards for all three are in Case materials.

Start with a task sentence

Too broad

“Analyze rural referrals.”

Specific and checkable

“Using the supplied synthetic logs, collect documented referral events through the scenario cutoff and preserve the source of each event.”

Then state who will use the output, which records are eligible, the reference period (the dates the records cover), and what the finished table looks like. For Problem 2, the essential fields are the referral identifier, the event type, the event date, the planned appointment date when one is stated, and the location of the source line.

Do not add costs, clinical urgency, or estimated waiting times just because an AI assistant suggests them. They are not part of this task, and the sources do not provide them.

Let AI draft the plan, then review every field

Ask AI to draft the plan, not to invent missing operating data. Review every proposed field against the task. A useful field either captures needed information or explains the meaning and origin of that information.

Keep the record unit explicit: one event statement is not one patient. Statements that look like duplicates, or dates that conflict, can stay in the collected material. Course 2 sorts them out later. A task-specific plan like this is also the start of your documentation.

A light version of a documentation standard

The course uses a small set of ideas from the Data Documentation Initiative (DDI), an international standard for describing data. Here, that simply means linking five things: the task, the question or extraction instruction, the variable, the source, and the collected record. These links make the handoff from Course 1 to Course 2 work. You do not need to produce any DDI technical files [2].

Start with one problem and the prefilled workpad. Completing all three problems on your own is an extended lab, not part of the one-hour core.

AI instruction for this stage

Prompt to adapt: draft the collection plan

Using task card P1, P2, or P3 and only its supplied sources, draft a collection plan: task, intended user, eligible records, reference period, what one row represents, required fields, and purpose of each field. Separate available sources from proposed follow-up sources. Do not supply data values or recommend clinical actions. Mark unspecified details for review.

Practice activity

Try it: Workpad A

Choose one task card. Keep four to six substantive fields, plus the identifiers and source references you need. Write down what one row represents in Workpad A.

Check your understanding

Which is the best collection task for Problem 2?

  1. Ask AI to explain all causes of rural referral delays.
  2. Extract documented events and dates from the supplied logs, with source locations and a stated cutoff.
  3. Ask AI to estimate missing completion dates.
Show the answer

Answer: B. It defines a limited, source-based output that can be checked and handed to Course 2.

A is too broad for the assigned dataset. C turns missing evidence into invented events. Begin with a specific source, record unit, and output.
