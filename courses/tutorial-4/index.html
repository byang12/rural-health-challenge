Read the transcript

Imagine the only clinic in a county of about 7,300 people. That was the size of Greene County, Alabama, in 2023. Everyone in the county depends on that one building, its few staff, and its open hours. Like any business, the clinic has to cover its costs every month. So why is a clinic like this harder to keep open than a clinic in a city?

A small-town clinic pays for the same things as a city clinic: a building, medical equipment, licensed staff, and a computer system for health records. People in business call these fixed costs, because they stay about the same whether ten patients come in or fifty. In a city, many patients share those costs. In a small town, far fewer patients share them, because fewer people live nearby. So each visit has to carry a larger share of the bill.

A small clinic also has fewer staff. If it has one nurse practitioner, a nurse with extra training who can examine patients and prescribe medicine, the clinic has to close for the day when that person is sick. Its patients also travel farther, so when a service closes, the trip to the next one grows longer.

These pressures show up in the money. Since January 2005, 197 rural hospitals have closed or stopped keeping patients overnight. In a 2026 national report, about 4 in 10 rural hospitals were losing money on their day-to-day operations. In the same report, the middle rural hospital had an operating margin of 2 percent. That means for every 100 dollars it earned from running its services, about 2 dollars were left after paying its costs.

This is a long-running problem. Congress created Rural Health Clinics in 1977, Critical Access Hospitals in 1997, and Rural Emergency Hospitals in 2020, and rural hospitals kept closing after each one. A service plan that works in a small town has to work where patients, staff, and money are all scarce.

Keep three ideas. A rural clinic pays city-level costs with fewer patients. It runs with few staff and thin margins. And when it closes, the next one is farther away. A rural clinic cannot offer everything a city hospital offers. It has a few staff, a small building, and a limited budget. So every rural clinic chooses which services to offer, and federal rules shape those choices.

Rural care happens at four types of sites set by federal rules. A Rural Health Clinic gives everyday care for common illness and prevention, with a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or nurse midwife seeing patients at least half the hours it is open. A Federally Qualified Health Center gives care to everyone, whatever they can pay, with lower fees for lower incomes. A Critical Access Hospital is a small hospital with up to 25 beds, more than a 35-mile drive from another hospital. A Rural Emergency Hospital gives emergency care around the clock, with no overnight beds.

The newest type shows how a service plan can change. Congress created the Rural Emergency Hospital in 2020, and hospitals could become one starting in 2023. A small hospital that can no longer afford to keep patients overnight can stop overnight care and keep 24-hour emergency care, so the town still has a place to go at three in the morning.

A small clinic can also offer care it has no specialist for. Telehealth means care by video or phone, and it removes the trip for some visits. A 2026 study of health centers that serve everyone, whatever they can pay, found that remote telehealth did more to improve depression screening and follow-up where transportation barriers were greater.

Another model coaches the local clinic. Project ECHO uses video sessions in which specialists help rural primary care clinicians treat patients with complex illnesses at their own clinic. In its first study, published in 2011, 407 patients with hepatitis C, a virus that damages the liver, were treated at a specialist clinic or at 21 rural and prison sites in New Mexico. About the same share cleared the virus at both: 58.2 percent at the local sites and 57.5 percent at the specialist clinic. Project ECHO now reports programs in 215 countries and areas.

Keep three ideas. Each site type offers a different set of services. A hospital can change its services to stay open. And video care and coaching can bring a specialist's skill to a small clinic. Four types of rural sites

Federal rules shape the menu

A rural clinic cannot offer everything a city hospital offers. It has a few staff, a small building, and a limited budget, so it chooses which services to offer. These four types are set by federal rules.

Rural Health Clinic

Everyday care for common illness and prevention. A nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or nurse midwife, clinicians who are not doctors but can examine and treat patients, sees patients at least half the hours it is open.

Federally Qualified Health Center

Care for everyone, whatever they can pay, with lower fees for lower incomes. Qualified means it meets federal rules for special Medicare and Medicaid payments.

Critical Access Hospital

A small hospital with up to 25 beds, more than a 35-mile drive from another hospital.

Rural Emergency Hospital

Emergency care around the clock, with no overnight beds. Congress created this type in 2020, and hospitals could convert starting January 1, 2023.

Read the transcript

A clinic can have caring staff and a full waiting room and still close. A clinic's business model is its plan for which services it offers, who uses them, who pays, and how those payments cover the costs. For a rural clinic, that plan decides whether a service lasts.

In this tutorial, a sustainable clinic is one that keeps needed care available over time. It needs four things at the same time: enough staff, enough money, enough patients, and safe quality. If a clinic loses any one of the four, a service can close. For example, a clinic cannot open without a nurse, however many patients it has.

Two government programs pay for much of this care. Medicare is federal health insurance mainly for people 65 and older. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that covers many people with low incomes. Private insurance and patients also pay. Each site type is paid by its own rules. For example, Medicare pays a Rural Health Clinic a flat amount for each visit, and pays a Critical Access Hospital its costs plus 1 percent. A Federally Qualified Health Center gets a set national amount adjusted for local prices, and a Rural Emergency Hospital gets a fixed monthly payment on top of its visit payments.

A service needs two kinds of money. A grant pays to start a service, and business students may know this as seed money. The service lasts only if payments for each visit cover its costs after the grant ends.

Here is a made-up example. Suppose an evening shift costs 600 dollars, and each visit brings in 100 dollars. Divide the cost by the payment, and the clinic needs 6 visits a night to break even, which means covering its costs with nothing left over. With 4 visits, the shift loses 200 dollars every night it opens.

The federal Rural Health Transformation Program gives states 50 billion dollars from 2026 to 2030, and Alabama received 203.4 million dollars for the first year. But the fund is not meant to pay day-to-day operating costs, and states may use at most 15 percent of it for payments straight to clinics and hospitals. So any new service still needs a way to be paid after the grant ends. Now try it: in the made-up example, if each visit brought in 80 dollars, how many visits would the shift need?

Read the transcript

Imagine a 68-year-old woman with no car who lives 40 minutes from town. She is a made-up example. Her primary care clinic, where she gets everyday care, says she needs a heart doctor. The clinic has no heart doctor on staff, so it sends her to a specialist in another town. This is called a referral.

For a clinic, a referral is a business process with six steps. First, the clinician decides she needs a specialist. Second, staff gather her records and insurance details. Third, they send the packet, and the specialist's office books a visit. Fourth, she travels to the visit or connects by video. Fifth, the specialist sees her. Sixth, the results go back to her clinic, where someone, such as a nurse, follows up. Health systems call that last step closing the loop.

Many referrals never finish. At one rural clinic in 2015, 10 primary care providers made 3,814 referrals in six months, and nearly half of the referred patients did not make the specialist appointment. A study of a large health system found that two steps explained much of such losses: booking the visit and getting there.

Step 3 breaks when no visit gets booked. In that large health system, in 2015 and 2016, 38.9 percent of tries to book a specialist visit never got an appointment date, and in all only 34.8 percent ended in a visit recorded as completed. Long waits and long distances explained much of that gap.

Step 4 breaks when the patient cannot get there. In 2017, 5.8 million people in the United States delayed medical care because they had no transportation. In 2023, about 22 percent of adults in Greene County, Alabama, had no reliable transportation, compared with 10.5 percent in Jefferson County, home of Birmingham. Both rates are adjusted for age, so a county with many older people does not look worse only because its people are older. These numbers do not show what residents already do to help each other get to care, so a team has to ask them, for example who gives rides now. Telehealth, care by video or phone, removes the trip for some visits.

Six steps of a referral

A business process across two offices

A referral is how a primary care clinic, the clinic people visit for everyday care, sends a patient to a specialist. For the clinic, it is a business process: steps that staff in two offices must finish in order.

Decide

02

Gather

Staff gather records, test results, and insurance details into a packet.

03

Send and book

The packet goes by fax, phone, or computer, and the specialist's office books a visit.

04

Get there

The patient travels or connects by video.

05

Visit

The specialist sees the patient.

06

Close the loop

The results go back to the first clinic, where someone, such as a nurse or a referral coordinator, follows up.

Watch first: Where to find data, and how AI can help

2:48. AI-generated voice. Captions are in the player's CC menu. Watch on YouTube

Read the transcript

You can study how rural clinics are run and paid before you collect anything yourself. Public records show what clinics and hospitals spend, what they earn, and which ones are in trouble.

Medicare cost reports, which hospitals, Rural Health Clinics, and health centers file every year, record what each one spends to run its services. HRSA, the Health Resources and Services Administration, collects data every year from health centers in its program on their patients, staff, costs, and revenues, in its Uniform Data System. Both are published as downloadable data, so students who code can load them into a spreadsheet or a program.

Other sources track the money pressure on rural hospitals. The Flex Monitoring Team publishes the middle values, called medians, for 30 financial measures of Critical Access Hospitals. The Sheps Center keeps a list of every rural hospital that has closed or stopped keeping patients overnight since 2005. It also ranks rural hospitals by their risk of financial trouble within two years. In 2025, 9 of the 46 Alabama hospitals it scored, about 1 in 5, were in the highest-risk group, the largest share of any state.

Each source has limits. The Uniform Data System covers only health centers in the federal program, and the Flex measures cover only Critical Access Hospitals. A high risk score means a higher chance of trouble; it does not say a hospital will close. And no record shows what local staff and patients know, so a team still has to ask them.

An AI chatbot can do three jobs with these sources. It can explain a financial term, such as operating margin. It can write a spreadsheet formula for the cost of each visit, or for the visits needed to break even. And it can summarize a long financial report.

But an AI chatbot can also give wrong numbers, or links that do not exist. So check every answer in three ways. First, open the link and confirm that the page is the original source. Second, find the number on that page and confirm that it matches. Third, confirm that the year and the measure match too. And never paste private patient information into an AI tool. Only the source can confirm a number

Only the original source can confirm a number, its year, and its measure. Asking an AI tool again repeats its guess. Rounding to the nearest 10 percent would turn the 2.0 percent median operating margin in the 2026 national report into 0 percent.
