Course 6 · Topic 5 of 5

Using AI to document, draft, and present

Reading time: about 10 minutes

In this topic

Use AI to turn rough notes from each work session into dated Team Log entries, then turn the log into proposal drafts, slide outlines, and question practice, while checking every fact and number.

You will leave with a Team Log, AI-assisted drafts of your proposal and slides, and a two-sentence statement of how you used and checked AI.

Read this part early

This is the last reading, but its first half is most useful at your first team meeting. If your team is early in the challenge, read Why write as you go and The Team Log now, and start your log today.

Why write as you go

Writing a 4–5 page proposal is hard work, and much of the difficulty comes from trying to remember weeks of work the week before submission. Why did we drop the county data? When did the handling time change, and why? Which AI output did we check, and how? Within a few days, those reasons are gone.

So writing should start when the investigation starts, not after it ends. That used to mean a lot of extra effort. With AI it takes far less: you supply rough notes after each work session, and AI turns them into a tidy, dated entry. AI makes the formatting easy. Capturing what actually happened is still your job, and it takes about five minutes.

The Team Log

The Team Log is a short, shared, dated document your team keeps from orientation to submission. Keep it wherever your team already works, such as a shared document.

  • It is working notes, not a fourth deliverable. You do not submit it, and it is not graded. Its job is to feed your proposal, supporting artifact, and slides.
  • It holds what your other records do not: the decisions you made and why, the options you dropped, the numbers that changed, how you used and checked AI, and your open questions.
  • It links instead of copying. Details of each input stay in your Course 1 information plan and Course 2 data notes. The log points to them.

What one entry contains

Write one entry per work session, about 100 words, by a teammate who takes the role in rotation. Date it on the day you write it.

The six fields of a Team Log entry
FieldWhat to write
1. Date and who attendedFor example, “Week 3; all three members.”
2. What we didOne or two lines, with links to the files or versions you changed.
3. What we foundKey numbers with units, each labeled observed, estimated, assumed, or modeled, with its source.
4. What we decided and whyInclude options you considered and dropped, and the reason.
5. AI useThe task, the tool, how you checked the result, and any correction or limitation; or “none.”
6. Open questionsThe weakest point right now and what would resolve it.
Practice case: a sample entry
Date and who attended
Week 4; all three members.
What we did
Tested a less favorable assumption from Course 4 in our spreadsheet.
What we found
At 10 minutes per packet instead of 7 (assumed), the modeled time saving disappears, while the $20 weekly subscription remains.
What we decided and why
Keep 7 minutes as the base case, and report 10 minutes as the breaking point in Section 5 and on slide 6.
AI use
AI re-ran the comparison table. Checked by hand: 20 × 10 = 200 minutes, the same as manual handling.
Open questions
What review time would clinic staff actually observe?

Whenever an input or result changes, add a row. The last column shows where the new number has been updated.

Four steps from notes to presentation

  1. Capture

    Rough notes become a log entry after every session.

  2. Draft

    The log becomes a decision-first proposal.

  3. Convert

    The proposal becomes a slide storyboard and speaker notes.

  4. Rehearse

    AI plays a skeptical judge, aimed at your real weak spots.

Step 1. Capture: rough notes to a log entry

At the end of each work session, the teammate on log duty gathers the raw material: bullet notes, a voice-memo transcript, the AI chat you used, or a sentence about what changed in the spreadsheet. Paste it into an AI tool with the prompt below. A teammate who was there reads the entry and corrects it before it goes into the log.

Prompt to adapt: capture

Format the notes below as one Team Log entry with these six fields: date and who attended; what we did; what we found; what we decided and why; AI use; open questions. Use only information in the notes. Keep every number and unit exactly as written. If a field is not covered, write “not recorded” and do not fill it in. Keep the entry under 100 words.

[Paste your notes here.]

Step 2. Draft: Team Log to proposal

Give AI your Team Log and the five section headings from the proposal template. Ask it to sort the entries into sections and draft decision-first (Topic 1). Your log is in time order, and your proposal must not be (the mystery novel pitfall in Topic 3). Reordering is something AI does well.

Prompt to adapt: draft

Here is our Team Log and the five section headings of our proposal. Sort each log entry under the section it supports. Then draft Section 1, starting with our recommendation in three sentences: the proposed change, the strongest evidence, and the next validation step. Use only facts and numbers from the log, and mark anything missing as [MISSING]. Write for a rural clinic manager with no technical background.

Once you have a draft, AI can also rewrite dense technical, statistical, or medical paragraphs in plain language, or help trim a six-page draft to the 4–5 page limit. Afterward, check that trimming did not remove a limitation or change a number.

Step 3. Convert: proposal to slides

Paste your finished proposal sections into AI and ask for the six-slide storyboard from Topic 2.

Prompt to adapt: convert

Using only the proposal below, draft a six-slide storyboard. For each slide, give a headline that states the point in one sentence, up to three short bullets, and what the audience should see. Then write speaker notes for three speakers: slides 1–2 (about 2½ minutes, about 300 words), slides 3–5 (about 3 minutes, about 360 words), and slide 6 (about 2½ minutes, about 300 words). Do not add any number that is not in the proposal.

Step 4. Rehearse: practice judges’ questions

Give AI your draft proposal together with the log’s open questions and number-change table, and ask it to play a skeptical judge. The log points its questions at your real weak spots. Check each answer you rehearse against the log, not against what the AI suggests.

Prompt to adapt: rehearse

Act as a skeptical judge with experience running a rural clinic. Based on the proposal and notes below, ask me one question at a time about our weakest assumption, the quality of our data, how we checked AI, and what would change our recommendation. After each of my answers, tell me what was unclear or unsupported.

Starting late?

If your team reaches Course 6 without a log, spend about 20 minutes building a short one: your three to five biggest decisions, every number that changed, and each AI use you can confirm from chat history or file versions. Label it “reconstructed in Course 6” so no one mistakes it for notes kept at the time.

Four documentation pitfalls

Pitfall: invented progress

AI fills gaps with plausible detail, so an entry describes a check nobody ran or a reason nobody gave.

Instead: the capture prompt tells AI to write “not recorded” for anything missing, and a teammate who was there approves each entry.

Pitfall: the night-before log

Writing the whole log just before submission. The reasons are already forgotten, and the log only repeats the proposal.

Instead: a short entry dated on the day beats a polished reconstruction.

Pitfall: the leaky summary

Pasting partner files or patient details into a public AI tool to “summarize today’s work.”

Instead: apply the same privacy rules to log entries as to everything else (rule 2 below).

Pitfall: the bloated log

AI-padded entries that no one rereads.

Instead: keep each entry to about 100 words. The log is useful only if your team can scan it in minutes.

Three golden rules for using AI

  1. Never let AI invent numbers, citations, or progress. AI organizes and polishes your team’s own notes, verified calculations, and sources. Check every number and citation in an AI-polished draft against your spreadsheet and the original sources, and have a teammate who was present approve every log entry.
  2. Protect partner privacy. Use only public, aggregate, or fictional materials with external AI tools. Never paste restricted partner files or identifiable patient information into a public AI service, including when you format log entries. Ask the data and GIS lead before using restricted partner material with any external AI service.
  3. Record AI use as you go, then report it. Each time AI supports an important step, add an AI-use entry: the task, the tool, the date, how you checked the result, and any correction or limitation. The two sentences in your proposal about how AI helped and how you verified the final numbers then come from these entries rather than from memory.
Practice case: a two-sentence AI statement

“We used [tool name, month] to check fictional referral packets against the clinic’s eight-item checklist and to help draft and shorten this proposal. We compared every AI answer item by item with the answer key, sorted errors into misses and false alarms, timed the whole job including checking, and checked every number in the proposal against our spreadsheet.”

Practice activity

Try it: your first entry

Write your first Team Log entry today. Paste your notes from your most recent work session into the capture prompt, check the result with a teammate, and save it with today’s date. Then add a row to the change table for any number that has changed since you started.

Check your log

  • Does each entry have a date and a teammate’s approval?
  • Does the change table show where each new number has been updated?
  • Did AI add anything that is not in your notes?
  • Is every important AI use recorded with its task, tool, date, check, and limitation?
